# HCI positioning: communicating consequences after authority changes

**Scope.** This is a focused review of primary, human-facing HCI and usable-security work relevant to AuthorityLens. It is intended to position a research question, not to serve as a systematic literature review. I inspected study methods, results, and stated limitations where a full paper was available. The sources below cover permission awareness and revocation, access reviews, delegated software agents, and human oversight of web agents.

## Position in the literature

**Assessment: partially studied; the combined post-control question is not clearly established in the reviewed sources.**

Prior work studies important components:

- Users often misunderstand or do not attend to app permissions, and permission settings are difficult to assess accurately.
- Revocation can fail to propagate to websites or third parties, leaving consent-related state in place.
- Access-review interfaces can help people make revocation decisions by showing context about access.
- Agent research studies user oversight, approval, privacy controls, and visibility of consequential actions during execution.
- A project agenda specifically asks about users’ expectations versus actual consequences of privacy-permission revocation.

These are substantial antecedents, so AuthorityLens should not claim that permissions, revocation, delegation, or human oversight are unstudied. In the bounded review below, however, I did not identify a participant study that compares a conventional **target-local permission indicator** with a provenance-preserving, system-level explanation of what remains possible after a local revoke action—especially when the remaining state includes independently active descendants, queued effects, or an unverified external principal.

Among the reviewed participant studies, none directly tests whether a user understands all three post-revocation consequences: downstream agent authority, already-pending effects, and unknown remote state. The nearest human-facing work addresses adjacent pieces (permission comprehension, revoke decisions, access-review context, or oversight during execution). The web-consent study by Kancherla et al. measures downstream propagation technically, without participants; the PrEvoke page describes a related project agenda, but is not a participant-results paper. That statement describes this review, not the entire literature: it does **not** establish that no such study exists or that the contribution is novel. A systematic search and citation-chaining review would be needed before making a priority claim.

Recent technical proposals further limit broad interface-novelty claims. Abak's September 2026 individual IETF Internet-Draft requires distinctions among control dispatch, target receipt, enforcement, and observed control effect, and says unconfirmed outcomes should remain visible to operational policy or human-notification behavior. Its defined “control effect” means the runtime-state change caused by the control, not the action's business effect; a separate freeze-race fixture records one in-flight external effect as UNKNOWN. The draft defines neither a user interface nor a human study, and is work in progress without formal IETF standing. This is adjacent evidence that truthful control-outcome reporting is already discussed, not evidence that people understand such a representation or that it solves AuthorityLens's combined pending-effect question.

## Coverage boundary

This is a purposive, focused review rather than a systematic review. It samples primary work located for permission comprehension and revocation, access review, account/API delegation, software-agent control, and human oversight of web agents. It is not an exhaustive search across all HCI, usable-security, privacy, IAM, and agent venues; no preregistered protocol, comprehensive database-by-database query log, or exhaustive backward/forward citation chase was used. Full papers were reviewed where available; one especially close adjacent item (PrEvoke) is represented only by its project page. Treat the synthesis as a positioning aid and verify coverage systematically before asserting novelty.

### Agent-permission systems and simulated user involvement

Brigham, Bagdasarian, Kohno, and Roesner’s **“Janus: a Playground for User-Involved Agentic Permission Management”** (2026, arXiv preprint) is a close artifact-level precedent. It provides a harness for comparing agent permission policies across six assistants and evaluates three scenarios using deterministic synthetic user responders and repeated runs. The inspected evaluation is not a human-participant study, and it focuses on permission decisions and policy violations rather than communicating post-revocation descendants, queued effects, and unresolved remote state. [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01510).

Michael and Roesner’s **“How Agents Ask for Permission”** (2026, arXiv preprint) surveys 21 agent-permission proposals and walks through permission handling in five commercial agents. It establishes that agent permission interfaces and revocation behavior are already an active research topic, but is not a controlled user-comprehension comparison of post-revoke consequences. [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13718).

### A particularly close project agenda

The project page for **“PrEvoke – Supporting Users in Informed Privacy Permission Revocation”** describes studying users’ expectations about what revoking a permission will do versus the actual resulting service behavior. [Project page](https://www.rz.unibw-muenchen.de/usable-security-and-privacy-en/research/projekte/prevoke). This is a close conceptual neighbor and should be acknowledged in positioning. In this review, I found the project page but not a corresponding primary results paper to assess; the page is evidence of a related research agenda, not evidence of a particular study result. Its described focus is app/service functionality after privacy-permission revocation, rather than surviving authority in a multi-agent delegation chain, pending effects, or uncertainty about a remote principal.

## What the gap can responsibly say

A defensible research question is:

> After a user revokes a target agent’s permission, what information should an interface communicate about (1) who can still initiate a new action, (2) which already-queued effects may still complete, and (3) which external states remain unverified?

A useful comparison is a fair target-local permission indicator versus a consequence-oriented view generated from the same backend state. The former answers a local question about the target principal; the latter can expose system-level descendants, pending effects, and uncertainty. The comparison should not make the local view deliberately inaccurate or imply that an empty local permission indicator proves system-wide revocation.

For a later human study, one could test whether participants distinguish **newly initiable actions** from **already-pending effects**, identify residual delegated access and its provenance, and calibrate answers when remote revocation is unconfirmed. Useful outcomes would include fact comprehension, false claims of complete revocation, confidence calibration, response time, and perceived effort. These would test human understanding; factual coverage in a benchmark alone cannot do so.

## Claims to avoid

- Do not claim this literature proves users misunderstand AuthorityLens’s specific state model.
- Do not claim no one has studied permission revocation consequences or agent oversight.
- Do not describe technical propagation audits as studies of user comprehension.
- Do not treat intention to delegate as observed delegation behavior or “delegation regret.”
- Do not infer reduced cognitive load, safety, or user preference from a structured-fact coverage score.
- Describe the proposed combined construct as **not clearly identified in this focused review**, pending a systematic literature search.
